# Session 6: Weekly Activity Calendar & Emotional Development ("Bhaav")
*Structuring daily/weekly activities across all developmental areas, then a deep dive into emotions — the law of conservation of emotion, the body's stress signals, and how to actually reduce tantrums*

## Overview
The session opens with a colourful sensory-art activity (thick paint dots sealed in a zip-lock bag for mess-free 'squish painting') and then helps parents organise everything learned so far into a simple weekly calendar: what to do every day (physical + a little mental/cognitive), what to do a few times a week (brain and cognitive challenges), and what to reserve for weekends (messy sensory play).

The main topic is emotional development — described as the deepest and most important remaining layer after brain, senses, body, mind, and cognition. The trainer introduces the idea that emotions, like energy, cannot be created or destroyed — only converted or expressed — and explains how suppressed emotions show up physically in the body. The session then gives a complete, practical sequence for handling a child's big emotions (name it, offer a choice, stay calm, use eye contact) and explains why techniques like forced 'sorry' don't work before a certain age.

## Key Points
### Zip-lock 'squish painting' sensory activity
- Thick dots of different paint colours are placed on paper, sealed inside a zip-lock bag, and the child mixes/presses the colours from outside the bag — mess-free but rich sensory and visual input.
- Benefits highlighted: visual stimulation from many colours at once, the sensation of touch/pressure without paint actually touching the skin, and 'animism' — the wonder of watching colours move and combine unpredictably.
- The finished artwork can be preserved (dated and pasted into the child's memory/drawing book) as one of their first pieces of art.
- Stamping (with okra, onion, potato halves) is suggested as a similar-value alternative activity.

### Building a simple weekly activity calendar
- **Every day (non-negotiable):**
  - Physical/body development — one activity a day (massage, carrying weight, climbing, crawling, pushing/pulling).
  - A one-line 'mind' moment — e.g., naming the day ('Mindful Monday'), which builds the sequencing concept over time.
- **A few times a week:**
  - Mental development games — universal concepts like colours, shapes, opposites, same/different.
  - Brain-stimulation activities (from the original 10 brain activities) — done consciously, twice a week is enough.
  - Cognitive development — problem-solving opportunities happen naturally within body/mind activities, so a dedicated slot isn't required, but can be added (e.g., opening a zip-lock bag halfway and letting the child finish).
- **Weekends (compulsory, messier sensory play):**
  - Flour, noodles, clay, mud, aloe gel, paint, paper tearing, kitchen-band sound play.
- Best timing guidance: physical activity that involves lifting/effort works well in the evening (helps sleep); sensory and mental activities should be done in the first half of the day, not at night, since they over-stimulate the brain; cognitive activities can be done any time; brain-development activities work best only when the child is fresh/playful.

### The law of conservation of emotions
- Just as energy cannot be created or destroyed — only converted from one form to another — emotions cannot be eliminated on command; they can only be expressed, suppressed, or converted.
- Telling a child (or yourself) to 'stop being angry' or 'control it' doesn't work, because you cannot simply erase an emotion — you can only choose how it's expressed.
- **Suppressed emotions tend to show up physically in the body:**
  - Headaches — suppressed, unexpressed anger.
  - Neck and mid-back pain — feeling overburdened or unsupported.
  - Chest pain — feeling suffocated, guilty, or hurt in a relationship.
  - Hip/lower-back pain — feeling 'stuck,' unable to move forward in some part of life.
  - Front-of-body pain generally — anxiety about the future.
  - Shoulder pain — wanting to do something you feel unable to do.
  - Itchy/aching feet — feeling directionless.
  - Heavy hands — struggling to let go of something in life.
- The trainer connects this to why women in particular may carry weight/health issues around the abdomen during high-stress periods — describing it as the body 'protecting' vital organs under chronic stress (shared as her personal understanding, not stated as established medical fact).

### Anger, disagreement, and other emotions are not the problem
- Anger itself is not bad — yelling out of anger, or dumping it on someone else, is the actual problem. Disappointment, disagreement, jealousy, and feeling hurt are also not problems in themselves; blaming others, disrespecting others, or trying to control others is where harm happens.
- 'Stubborn' children are reframed as 'determined' children — determination just needs direction, not suppression; a child's persistence in early years is a strength, not a flaw to be eliminated.

### Why 'sorry' often doesn't work before age 7–8
- True 'sorry' requires introspection — understanding what you did wrong and genuinely regretting it — and a child's brain cannot reliably introspect before roughly age 8.
- Forcing repeated 'sorry, sorry' teaches the child to say the word to end the parent's displeasure ('verbal vomit'), not to actually reflect on their behaviour.
- Similarly, 'thank you' before age 7–8 is typically pleasure-driven (thanking for a treat) rather than true gratitude — both are fine to gently model and encourage, but should never be forced or demanded.
- A more effective approach: acknowledge the child's feeling first ('looks like you're upset'), then guide the repair action ('let's go say sorry together') rather than commanding 'say sorry.'

### The core sequence for handling big emotions
- **1) Name the emotion:**
  - 'Looks like you're angry/sad' — this alone reduces a large part of a tantrum because the child feels understood.
- **2) Offer a simple choice:**
  - 'Red brush or yellow brush first?' — gives the child some sense of control, reducing power struggles.
- **3) Stay calm and keep eye contact:**
  - Never correct or explain an emotion without eye contact; a calm adult voice and face regulate the child's nervous system.
- Repetition matters: like other early instructions, this sequence typically needs to be repeated consistently for a while (weeks, not once) before it visibly reduces tantrums — patience is required, not perfection.

### Why children only 'listen' when a parent shouts
- If a parent repeats a calm request multiple times and only takes action once they raise their voice, the child learns that calm words are 'fake' and only a raised voice is meaningful — this is a trained pattern, not defiance.
- Breaking it requires consistency: say it calmly once or twice, and if action is needed, follow through calmly rather than escalating to a shout.

### Handling other adults' reactions (in-laws, relatives)
- When a relative scolds or disciplines a child in a different (perhaps harsher) style, don't badmouth them to the child — instead, help the child understand it as 'that's grandpa's way of talking, and there's love behind it' rather than framing the relative as simply 'difficult.'
- If a relative teaches something the parents actively disagree with (e.g., touching everyone's feet), have a private conversation with that adult and give the child a clear, calm alternative ('you can do it if you want, but it isn't something we require') rather than contradicting them in front of the child.

## Activities
- **Zip-Lock Squish Painting** — Mess-free colour-mixing sensory and visual stimulation using thick paint dots sealed in a bag.
- **Stamping with Vegetables** — Okra/onion/potato stamping gives similar visual/sensory value to squish painting.
- **Emotion Naming in the Moment** — Naming a feeling as it happens ('looks like you're excited') builds emotional vocabulary and reduces escalation.
- **Hug + Breathe** — A simple hug paired with a deep breath and reassurance ('mama loves you') calms and connects.
- **Calm Corner** — A designated spot (wash basin, prayer lamp, a hug from a partner) parents can retreat to briefly to regulate before returning to the child.
- **Mirror Faces** — Making different emotion faces together in a mirror builds recognition and vocabulary for feelings.
- **Comfort Object Talk** — Encouraging a child to hug a teddy bear when upset gives them a self-soothing tool.
- **Slow, Calm Voice Practice** — Demonstrates that a calm tone gets a completely different response than a raised voice, even with the same words.
- **Feeling Stories & Action-Feeling Songs** — Simple songs ('when you're happy and you know it...' variations for sad/scared/angry) pair emotions with physical actions.
- **Co-Regulation Hold** — Sitting quietly holding the child (without lecturing) lets your calm nervous system regulate theirs.
- **Labelling Parent's Own Emotions** — Saying 'mama is feeling tired/upset' out loud models emotional awareness and honesty for the child.
- **Predictable Routine + Visual Emotion Cards** — Visual emoji/emotion cards and predictable routines help children recognise and anticipate their own emotional states.

## Q&A
**Q: Why does my child only listen after I've said something 3–4 times and finally shout?**
A: You've unintentionally trained your child that calm words don't carry real consequence and only a raised voice does. Break this by following through calmly the first or second time instead of escalating to a shout — consistency, not volume, changes the pattern.

**Q: Should I make my child say sorry when they've hurt someone?**
A: Guide them through the action ('let's go say sorry together') rather than commanding 'say sorry' — before about age 8, true introspective apology isn't developmentally possible, so forcing the word teaches performance, not remorse.

**Q: My child has separation anxiety at daycare — what can I do?**
A: Use 'inform and perform': tell them daily and in advance what will happen ('mama will drop you and come back in the evening'), never sneak away without saying goodbye, and avoid interrogating them about their day ('what did you do today?') — ask warmly instead ('I had fun today, did you?'). Settling in usually takes 3–4 weeks; be patient and consistent.

**Q: Does keeping my child in daycare for long hours harm their development?**
A: It's not the number of hours that matters most, but whether the parent is genuinely present and attentive during the time they do have — specifically three fully-focused 20-minute windows: on waking, on reunion after separation, and before sleep.

## Key Takeaways
- Build a simple weekly rhythm: something physical every day, mental/brain challenges a few times a week, and messy sensory play reserved for weekends.
- Emotions cannot be erased on command — they can only be expressed in healthy ways or converted, so the goal is teaching healthy expression, not suppression.
- Persistent, unexplained physical symptoms (headaches, neck/shoulder/hip pain) may reflect emotions that are being suppressed rather than expressed.
- The reliable sequence for a child's big emotion: name the feeling, offer a small choice, stay calm, and keep eye contact — repeated consistently over time.
- Never force 'sorry' or 'thank you' as a script before roughly age 7–8 — model and guide the action instead of demanding the words.
- If your child only responds when you shout, it's because calm words have stopped carrying real consequence — restore that by following through calmly and consistently.

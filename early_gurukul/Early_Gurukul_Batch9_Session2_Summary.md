# Session 2: The Five Senses & the Vestibular System

*How natural sensory input builds the brain, plus the 'sixth sense' (balance and body-awareness)*

## Session Overview

Building on Session 1's brain science, this session covers the second major stage of a child's development: the senses. The trainer explains that everything a child's brain learns must first pass through one of the five senses — sight, sound, smell, taste, and touch — and that natural, everyday household inputs (spices, textures, sounds of different vessels, natural light) are far more valuable for brain-building than artificial toys, lights, or sounds.

The second half of the session introduces the vestibular (proprioceptive) sense — the child's 'sixth sense' for balance, direction, and body position — and links it to physical milestones like sitting, crawling, walking, and running. A large set of simple sensory activities across each sense is shared, along with guidance on praising process over outcome and managing sensory sensitivities and boredom.

## Key Points

### Senses are the only 'road' to the brain

- Information can only reach the brain through the five senses — without sight, sound, touch, taste, or smell involved, the brain has nothing to work with (illustrated with a blue pen example: with eyes closed and no touch, you cannot know its colour).
- Once senses collect information, the brain assigns meaning to it and stores it; repeated sensory experiences are what build lasting brain connections.
- Natural, everyday household items (spices, wet soil, bangles, payal, kitchen vessels) are ideal sensory inputs — they don't need to be purchased or artificial.

### Why sensory development matters

- Strengthens neural connections and improves attention and focus.
- Children with well-developed senses tend to have fewer tantrums and less 'sensory overload' seeking behaviour (e.g., throwing plates just to hear a new sound).
- Supports speech development, eating, and sleep.
- Builds emotional security — familiar sounds/smells (like a chant or bell) create a calming, grounding effect, much like an adult recalling a peaceful memory.
- Makes learning feel natural rather than forced.

### The five senses, one by one

- Sight (eyes):
  - New shapes, natural colours, faces, and movements are all fresh input; describing images in detail (not just naming objects) builds vocabulary and observation.
- Hearing (ears):
  - Different natural sounds (glass, steel, wood, cloth, water) — each new sound is processed as valuable new information.
- Smell (nose):
  - Natural smells (spices, flowers, wet soil, coffee, perfumes) build memory and emotional association strongly and quickly.
- Taste (tongue):
  - Direct exposure to distinct tastes (lemon, bitter gourd, honey, salt) supports healthy eating habits and taste-bud development; 'umami' introduced as a distinct taste category (paneer, chicken).
- Touch (skin) — called the single most powerful sense:
  - Builds safety, bonding, and calmness more than any other sense.

### The vestibular/proprioceptive sense ('sixth sense')

- This is the child's sense of where their body is in space — balance, direction, and control while moving.
- It explains normal variation in milestones: e.g., neck-holding/sitting is considered normal anywhere from 5th to 9th month because it depends on this sense developing.
- Age-linked vestibular milestones:
  - 9–12 months: crawling in various styles, standing while holding support, enjoying gentle rolling/swinging.
  - 1–1.5 years: walking independently, sitting like a 'potty' pose to pick up toys, pushing toys while walking, climbing low furniture.
  - 18 months+: running with more confidence, kicking a ball (needs judging speed/distance), climbing and descending stairs.
  - 2–3 years: jumping on one leg, walking on toes, balancing games, confident climbing on playground equipment.
- The stronger this sense, the more confident the child becomes physically and emotionally.

### Praising the process, revisited

- Appreciate the quality/effort behind an action ('you focused so well') rather than the outcome ('you finished the puzzle') — the child then repeats the quality (focus, care, organisation) in many areas of life, not just that one task.
- Avoid over-praising every tiny action during play — let the child stay absorbed; step in with recognition only occasionally, especially after real effort.

### Sensory sensitivities and boredom

- It's normal for some children to dislike certain textures (e.g., wet dough, flour) — this sensory sensitivity typically fades between ages 3 and 5; don't force it, gently increase exposure over time (e.g., mix the texture into hand-play indirectly).
- Rotate toys in small labelled boxes (5–7 toys per box, swapped every few days) rather than giving access to everything — this helps children learn to enjoy 'boredom' and reduces the constant demand for something new.

## Activities Taught in This Session

| Activity | Purpose / How |
|---|---|
| Barefoot Texture Path | Walking barefoot on different natural/household textures (soil, grass, towel) releases calming 'alpha waves' and builds tactile awareness. |
| Oil + Flour Sensory Massage / Play Dough | A warm-oil-and-flour paste gives a new touch texture for massage or play. |
| Water Transfer / Sponge Squeeze | Squeezing a wet sponge between containers builds grip strength useful later for writing and eating. |
| Mystery Touch Box | Child touches and identifies hidden objects (lemon, sponge, wooden spoon) by feel alone — builds tactile discrimination and reduces fear of unknown textures. |
| Hot/Cold Touch Play | Exposing the child (safely) to warm and cool objects/cloths teaches the vocabulary and sensation of temperature. |
| Grain Digging Tray | Hiding toys in a tray of rice/grain for the child to find by touch — builds tactile search skills. |
| Mud/Soil Play | Natural soil play (multani mitti etc.) builds tactile tolerance and comfort with 'messy' natural textures. |
| Soft vs Hard Sorting | Comparing cotton/feather vs stone/wood teaches touch vocabulary and reduces general touch-related fear. |
| Lamp/Diya Gazing & Bubble Watching | Looking at gentle natural light or bubbles trains eye-tracking and produces a calming brain-wave effect. |
| Shadow Story Play & Nature Colour Hunt | Storytelling with shadows/props and spotting shades of a colour outdoors builds visual attention and vocabulary. |
| Kitchen Orchestra & Loud-vs-Soft Sound Game | Tapping different vessels and contrasting loud/soft sounds teaches auditory discrimination and the actual meaning of 'talk softly.' |
| Sound Direction Play & Clap-and-Pause | Locating a bell's direction or responding to clap patterns builds auditory attention needed later for listening/focus. |
| Single-Taste & Texture Food Exposure | Offering one distinct taste/texture daily (lemon, honey, salt, mashed/crumbled food) builds a confident, adventurous eater over time. |

## Parent Q&A Highlights

**My baby gets scared of loud/deep sounds and covers her ears — how do I handle it?**
> Don't over-react or force the sound away; treat it matter-of-factly, gently reintroduce the sound in playful contexts (e.g., during a story), and trust that the child's response to sound varies and settles with time — it's not a permanent issue.

**My child refuses to play with flour/dough (throws it, won't touch it) but plays fine with mud, etc. Is something wrong?**
> This is 'sensory sensitivity' to specific textures, not a disorder — very common, and usually resolves between ages 3–5. Sit with the child, model playing with the texture yourself, and let them approach at their own pace rather than forcing contact.

## Key Takeaways

- All learning enters through the five senses — deliberately expose your child to natural, everyday sensory experiences rather than relying on toys or screens.
- Touch is the single most powerful sense for building a child's sense of safety and bonding.
- The vestibular ('sixth') sense — balance and body-awareness — underlies confidence in movement; explains normal variation in physical milestones.
- Praise the quality/effort behind an action, not just the outcome, so the child internalises helpful traits (focus, care, organisation).
- Sensory sensitivities to specific textures are normal and typically resolve by age 3–5 — don't force, just keep gently offering exposure.
- Rotate a small number of toys at a time rather than giving access to everything — this builds tolerance for 'boredom' and creative play.

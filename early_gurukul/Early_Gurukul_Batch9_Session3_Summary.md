# Session 3: Pistachio-Tree Craft, Emotional Vocabulary & the 'Stop' System

*Building an emotional vocabulary in young children, teaching real boundaries with 'No/Stop,' and starting physical (gross/fine motor) development*

## Session Overview

The session opens with a hands-on craft: parents make a pistachio-shell 'tree' with a traced hand-print on a drawing book. This single craft is later reused for three purposes — a sensory activity (pressing acupressure points on the hand/feet), a storytelling prop (birds/fish on the tree), and a pretend-play tool (drawing happy/sad/angry faces on the shells to build emotional vocabulary).

The middle of the session tackles one of the most common parent questions: how to say 'No' or 'Stop' effectively. The trainer introduces a consistent, calm, countdown-based method for setting boundaries without yelling, and distinguishes fear-based restrictions from genuinely dangerous situations. The session then transitions into physical development — how a child's gross motor (big muscle) and fine motor (small muscle) needs drive many 'annoying' behaviours like throwing, pulling, and kicking, and shares a large set of physical activities for both muscle groups.

## Key Points

### Pistachio-tree craft and its three uses

- Materials: pistachio shells, Fevicol, a drawing book, and a marker.
- Use 1 — Sensory:
  - Pressing the child's hand/feet onto the textured pistachio shells stimulates acupressure points and provides a new tactile input.
- Use 2 — Storytelling:
  - The tree and birds become props for spontaneous stories, building the child's sense of imagination and 'animism' (bringing inanimate objects to life through story).
- Use 3 — Pretend play & emotional vocabulary:
  - Drawing different facial expressions (happy, sad, angry, crying) on the shells gives the parent a tool to name and narrate emotions for the child.

### Building an emotional vocabulary early

- Children as young as a few months can absorb the names of emotions even before they can speak — 'mama is upset,' 'mama is angry,' 'that made me happy' — narrating your own emotions (and how you managed them) models emotional regulation.
- A child cannot yet say 'I feel overwhelmed' — they can only cry, so crying is often the only outlet available for many different feelings (sadness, hunger, missing someone, even excess happiness).
- Do not distract a child out of crying or dismiss it ('why cry over something so small?') — acknowledge the feeling ('I understand you feel bad, mama is here') while holding the boundary; this is more effective than distraction and preserves trust.
- Emotionally vocabulary-rich children tend to be calmer and more polite because they have a way to express feelings other than escalation.

### The 'No/Stop' system — teaching real boundaries

- Very young children (under ~3.5 years) do not understand 'No' as a clear instruction — it is vague and often triggers a shock/attention response in the amygdala rather than actual comprehension.
- The recommended method:
  - Say the instruction calmly and consistently ('Stop it now').
  - Repeat once more with the same calm tone.
  - On the third and final time, add a countdown: 'I will take it at the count of zero — 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0' — then calmly remove the object or redirect.
  - Never raise your voice, scream, or over-explain during this sequence — that adds 'fuel' (the trainer's word) to the child's emotional reaction.
- This exact sequence, repeated consistently (about 18+ times), teaches the child to respond to 'Stop' reliably without daily drama.
- Distinguish fear-based restrictions from real danger: don't stop a child from safely climbing (a 'safe risk') just because it worries you — only intervene firmly for genuinely dangerous situations (hot stove, boiling water), and even then, let the child safely feel/verify the danger (e.g., touch a cooker gently with a cloth) so the concept of 'hot' becomes real to them.
- Teasing/testing behaviour where a child repeats an action after being told 'no' is not deliberate manipulation — before roughly age 3.5, children can't yet plan strategic teasing; they are simply seeking a repeatable reaction (attention), which drama/over-reaction unintentionally rewards.

### Physical development — the body's 'hunger' for movement

- A child's muscles are described as 'hungry' — throwing, pulling, kicking, and squeezing are the body's way of demanding movement input, not misbehaviour to be punished.
- Two muscle categories:
  - Gross motor (big muscles): balance, posture, confidence — involved in climbing, running, pushing, carrying.
  - Fine motor (small muscles): writing, eating, buttoning — involved in squeezing, threading, scooping, pinching.
- Giving the child structured outlets for these movements (a ball to throw, a drawer to open/close, a rope to pull) reduces undesired versions of the same behaviour (throwing valuables, pulling hair).
- Benefits of sufficient physical activity: better brain-body connection, coordination and balance, stronger speech/jaw muscles (from muscle use elsewhere in the body), emotional release/calm, better focus, and fewer 'power struggles.'
- Whether a child is genuinely 'hyperactive' should be decided by a doctor, not assumed by parents — high energy and constant movement in early childhood is typically normal development, not a disorder.

### Milestones (approximate, 6 months–3 years)

- 6–9 months:
  - Rolls both ways; sits with little/no support by ~9 months; reaches for and grasps objects.
- 9–12 months:
  - Crawls; pulls to stand with support; picks up small objects (pincer grasp).
- 12–18 months:
  - Walks without support before 18 months; climbs onto furniture; holds spoon/cup; scribbles with a crayon.
- 2–3 years:
  - Jumps with both feet; climbs and descends stairs independently; throws a ball (catching develops later, needing separate eye-body coordination); turns book pages; builds towers; uses a spoon confidently.

## Parent Q&A Highlights

**How exactly do I say 'no' so my child understands and stops?**
> Use a fixed, calm phrase repeated up to three times, with a countdown on the final repetition (5,4,3,2,1,0), then calmly remove the item or redirect — never raise your voice or over-explain, and keep the exact same wording every time.

**My child keeps running back to something after I said no — is she teasing me?**
> No — children under about 3.5 years cannot yet plan deliberate teasing; she is 'gathering data' about what reaction she'll get. A big/dramatic reaction unintentionally rewards the repeat behaviour, so respond calmly and consistently instead.

**Should I stop my child from climbing furniture/stairs out of fear she'll fall?**
> Distinguish fear-driven restriction from real danger. If it's a 'safe risk' (like climbing supervised furniture), let her try with you nearby rather than blocking exploration — reserve firm 'no's for genuinely dangerous situations.

**My child cries a lot over 'small' things like a broken toy — should I distract her?**
> Don't distract or dismiss the crying — accept the feeling ('I understand you're sad the toy broke, that's okay to feel'), and where possible involve her in fixing it (e.g., taping the toy together) so she learns to value things and process disappointment rather than being rushed out of it.

## Key Takeaways

- Build your child's emotional vocabulary early by naming feelings — including your own — well before they can speak in full sentences.
- Use a consistent, calm, countdown-based 'Stop' routine instead of shouting or repeated vague 'No's — it works because it's predictable, not because it's forceful.
- Separate real danger from your own fear — allow supervised, safe risk-taking (like climbing) rather than blocking exploration out of anxiety.
- 'Hungry' muscles drive a lot of throwing/pulling/kicking behaviour — give your child structured physical outlets instead of only saying 'stop.'
- Never let a doctor's diagnosis of 'hyperactive' be assumed by parents based on normal high energy — that judgment belongs to a professional.
- Accept and name a child's difficult emotions rather than distracting them away — this builds trust and long-term emotional regulation.

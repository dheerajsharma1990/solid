# Session 7: Social Development — Learning to Live and Relate to Others
*Milestones of social growth from 6 months to 2+ years, why 'sharing is caring' is the wrong lesson, and building trust, turn-taking, and gentle touch*

## Overview
This session opens with two detailed parent Q&As — how to respond when a baby falls or gets mildly hurt (react neutrally, don't overreact), and why babies copy the communication style of whoever they spend the most time with (because a child's brain, unable to self-direct for the first few years, learns almost entirely through copying) — before moving into the main topic: social development.

Social development is defined as everything a child learns through relationships: trusting others, taking turns, reading facial/emotional expressions, waiting, showing empathy, and cooperating. The session walks through social milestones from 6 months (social smile) to 2+ years (cooperative pretend play and early friendship), explains the brain science behind imitation (mirror neurons), and gives parents a large set of practical activities — with special attention to reframing 'sharing' as something that should be modelled and encouraged, never forced.

## Key Points
### Responding to falls and minor injuries
- For small, harmless falls, react neutrally or even playfully ('come on, up you get!') rather than rushing over dramatically — children take their emotional cue about how serious something is directly from the parent's reaction.
- Reserve real concern and attention for genuine injuries (bleeding, a big fall) — for everything else, staying calm teaches the child that falling and getting up again is a normal, unremarkable part of moving and exploring.

### Why children copy family members' communication style
- A child's brain cannot yet self-direct or filter for the first 3–3.5 years, so almost everything — tone, phrases, expressions — gets copied from whoever they spend the most time with, including nannies, grandparents, and older siblings.
- This is normal and not a cause for concern unless the copied content includes harmful language — in which case, react calmly rather than dramatically, since dramatic reactions to a 'bad word' often reinforce its use by giving it extra attention. Calmly note it's 'a different word' with less appeal than acceptable alternatives, and it typically fades within days.

### What social development actually covers
- Trusting another person, taking turns, waiting patiently, reading others' facial expressions and emotional states, expressing empathy, and cooperating with others (a skill that only exists in relation to someone else, unlike an individual skill).
- The goal isn't to make a child universally sociable or extroverted — introverted children can be just as socially developed; the focus is on the underlying skills of trust, turn-taking, empathy, and cooperation, however they're expressed.

### Social development milestones (approximate)
- **6–9 months:**
  - Develops a general 'social smile' at people; enjoys faces and human connection — an early building block of social bonding.
- **9–12 months:**
  - Stranger anxiety appears (crying with unfamiliar people, calm with familiar ones) — this is a healthy sign of attachment, not a problem to fix.
  - Shows clear preference for and closeness with familiar caregivers.
- **Around 12 months:**
  - Strong attachment behaviour — clinging tightly, wanting to be picked up repeatedly ('Vikram-Vetal phase') as a way of checking that love and safety are still there.
- **12–18 months:**
  - Parallel play — plays near another child or a busy parent without directly playing together, but watches and absorbs constantly (an important, valuable phase, not disengagement).
- **18–24 months:**
  - Imitation play begins — often reproducing a parent's words/actions later with a toy or teddy bear rather than immediately in front of the parent.
- **2 years+:**
  - Simple cooperative pretend play with others (mama-baby roleplay, feeding a doll together); early concept of friendship begins to form.

### Mirror neurons and modelling
- The brain's mirror-neuron system drives children to copy the behaviour of their primary caregivers almost automatically — this applies to both wonderful habits and unwanted ones, so a parent's own behaviour is the strongest teaching tool available.
- Children build trust and empathy specifically by experiencing loving, secure relationships firsthand — the more love and security they experience within the family, the more they expect and offer it in relationships outside the home.

### Language and choice reduce social friction
- As a child gains vocabulary and can name specific things they want, they cooperate better, feel more respected, and resist less — introduce specific everyday words (not just generic sounds) so children can express needs precisely.
- Choice of words is itself a form of social development: children who can say what they want (rather than only screaming) are far more likely to be understood and, in turn, more cooperative.

### Reframing 'sharing is caring'
- The trainer strongly cautions against the common phrase 'sharing is caring' — pointing out that adults themselves don't share everything they care about (e.g., a wedding ring), so it's an unfair standard to hold children to.
- **Recommended approach instead:**
  - Teach ownership first — 'this is mine, it can stay with me' is a legitimate feeling to have.
  - Model sharing yourself (offering something you're comfortable giving) so the child sees generosity demonstrated, not demanded.
  - Use turn-taking with a visible timer ('let's play until the alarm rings, then it's their turn') rather than forcing an immediate handover.
  - Praise the effort to consider sharing, even if the child ultimately decides not to.
- Not sharing does not mean a child doesn't care — connection and modelling build genuine cooperation over time; forcing it does not.

### Gentle touch and respecting family relationships
- A simple physical routine/song ('we love each other, we take care of each other') teaches children how much pressure is appropriate when they express affection, since toddlers often don't yet know how to modulate their strength.
- At least 14+ warm, gentle touches a day from parents (hugs, pats, holding hands) are associated with children feeling more secure and peaceful — this can be intentionally built into daily routines.
- Greetings (hi, bye, thank you) should be modelled consistently rather than forced — children pick them up naturally through repeated exposure.
- A simple hand-drawn 'family book' (photos or drawings of family members, labelled in large letters) helps a young child feel more secure in relationships and doubles as an early reading/eye-tracking exercise.

## Activities
- **Peekaboo Variations** — Hiding faces/objects and revealing them teaches interaction rhythm and can be extended by making different emotion faces on reveal.
- **Turn-Taking Ball Pass** — Passing a ball around a group while naming whose turn it is builds the concept of waiting and turn-taking.
- **Imitation Game ('Do What I Do')** — Simple copying games (roly-poly, flying kiss, funny faces) build observational imitation skills.
- **Home Tour / Room Tour** — A guided 3–5 minute narrated tour of one room (bedroom, kitchen) builds vocabulary and observational engagement.
- **Pretend Tea Party / Daycare Role Play** — Hosting toys at a 'tea party' or acting out a daycare routine indirectly teaches conversational turn-taking and eases transitions to real settings.
- **Gentle Touch Song ('We Love Each Other')** — A simple physical routine teaches appropriate pressure and affection expression.
- **Help-Me Tasks (Real Chores)** — Giving a child a genuine daily responsibility (watering a plant, putting away their own clothes) builds respect, ownership, and cooperation — described as more valuable than being 'helped' with praise.
- **Greeting Practice (Modelled, Not Forced)** — Saying hi/bye/thank you consistently around the child lets them absorb and eventually use greetings naturally.
- **Family Photo Book** — A simple labelled book of family members and their names/relationships builds security and early reading/eye-tracking skills.
- **Puppet Conversation** — A hand-drawn or finger puppet having a simple back-and-forth conversation models dialogue turn-taking.
- **Waiting Games ('Ready, Steady, Go')** — Structured waiting-then-action games build patience and impulse control in a fun format.
- **Group Circle Songs (e.g., Ring-a-Ring-a-Roses)** — Group songs that end in 'falling down' and laughing teach a calm, playful response to minor mishaps.

## Q&A
**Q: My child has become more attached to their father/grandmother lately and doesn't play with me as much — is that a problem?**
A: No — as children develop, they naturally explore connections with multiple trusted caregivers who feel safe to them, while still returning to their primary attachment figure (often mom) for comfort and sleep. This 'daddy-crazy' or 'grandma-crazy' phase is normal and can last on and off for years; join in as a partner in the activity rather than feeling replaced.

**Q: I work from home — should I make eye contact with my child while I'm on a call, or will they feel ignored?**
A: It's fine, even helpful, if the child sees you're focused on work and doesn't get your attention during that time — this teaches them to respect that you're occupied. What matters is that once your work/break is genuinely over, you are fully, warmly present and available, so the child learns there's a clear 'work time' and 'you time,' not that they're being ignored outright.

**Q: My daughter won't share her toys with her younger sibling — what should I do?**
A: Give each child their own labelled box of toys. The owner can choose to share from her box or not — that's her right — while the other child's box is naturally theirs to control. Sharing should be encouraged and modelled, never mandated, and ownership should be clearly respected on both sides.

**Q: My 10-month-old gets scared of dog barking/cow mooing even from a distance — how do I help?**
A: This is a normal reaction to an unfamiliar sound. Acknowledge it ('yes, that was a different sound, you got scared') and calmly go closer together to observe the source rather than avoiding it — repeated calm exposure (roughly 15–20 times) typically settles the fear; a single explanation won't instantly fix it.

## Key Takeaways
- React neutrally to small falls and bumps — your calm reaction teaches your child that these moments aren't a big deal.
- Children copy the communication style of whoever they spend the most time with; respond calmly (not dramatically) to anything undesirable they pick up, since overreacting often reinforces it.
- Stop teaching 'sharing is caring' — teach ownership first, model generosity yourself, and use visible turn-taking (like a timer) instead of forcing a handover.
- Social development milestones (social smile, stranger anxiety, parallel play, imitation play, cooperative play) unfold in a predictable sequence — trust the process rather than pushing a child ahead of it.
- Build in at least a dozen or more warm, gentle physical touches a day — they are strongly linked to a child's sense of security.
- Give your child real, ongoing household responsibilities (not just 'help') — it builds respect, ownership, and cooperative behaviour more effectively than instructions or praise alone.
